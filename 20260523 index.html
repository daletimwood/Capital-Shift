Series premise

Most financial content teaches tactics. This series teaches frameworks — the mental models that determine whether tactics stick. Each episode is built around a specific cognitive or structural shift, grounded in academic or practitioner research, and made actionable for a UK audience. Episodes can run sequentially as a curriculum, or be pulled forward when a news event provides a natural hook.

Primary intellectual sources

  • Thaler & Benartzi — Save More Tomorrow; behavioural finance and inertia
  • Granovetter — The Strength of Weak Ties (1973 & 1983)
  • William Bernstein — The Four Pillars of Investing
  • Kiyosaki — Rich Dad, Poor Dad (frameworks, not specifics)
  • Robert Putnam — Bowling Alone; social capital theory
  • FCA Financial Lives survey — UK-grounded data
  • Kahneman & Tversky — Prospect Theory; loss aversion
  • Morgan Housel — The Psychology of Money
How to use this document

Run episodes sequentially as a curriculum, starting with Pillar I. Alternatively, when a current event provides a hook — a Budget, rate change, market correction, pension reform — pull the relevant episode forward and use the "Current event hooks" tags as your bridge into the core lesson. Each episode is self-contained but cross-references adjacent modules.

01
Financial Literacy · Foundation
What Is an Asset? Most People Have It Backwards

Kiyosaki's most enduring contribution: an asset puts money in your pocket; a liability takes it out. Most people call their car and lifestyle "assets." This episode redraws the map.

Episode beats
  • The accounting definition vs. the cashflow definition — where they agree and where they diverge
  • Why your primary residence sits in an ambiguous middle ground (builds equity, but costs money monthly)
  • Mapping listeners' own "balance sheets" — the honest version
  • UK-specific: pension as the most tax-efficient asset most people ignore
Intellectual grounding

Kiyosaki's cashflow definition is directionally correct but departs from GAAP accounting. Anchor with Bernstein's Four Pillars on distinguishing income-generating from appreciating assets, and note the difference matters for strategy.

Behavioural Finance · Foundation
The Rat Race Is Real — and Salary Won't Save You

Why income growth alone doesn't build wealth. The treadmill of lifestyle inflation means most people never escape trading time for money, regardless of what they earn. The cashflow quadrant as a navigation tool.

Episode beats
  • Kiyosaki's cashflow quadrant: Employee → Self-employed → Business owner → Investor
  • Why moving across the quadrant is the goal — and pension investing gets you to the I quadrant sooner than you think
  • The lifestyle inflation trap: Kahneman's hedonic adaptation as the mechanism
  • UK data: FCA Financial Lives — percentage of high earners with negligible savings
Intellectual grounding

Kahneman & Tversky's hedonic adaptation; Morgan Housel's Psychology of Money on the goalpost that keeps moving. The quadrant is Kiyosaki's framework but the mechanism is well-supported behavioural science.

Investing · Savings Behaviour
Pay Yourself First — The One Rule That Beats Willpower

Kiyosaki's instruction to save and invest before paying expenses, not from whatever's left. Turns out the behavioural finance evidence is emphatic: automation defeats intention every time.

Episode beats
  • Why "I'll save what's left" always fails — the behavioural mechanism (present bias)
  • Thaler & Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow: the 3–4% savings rate improvement from automation alone
  • UK levers: pension auto-enrolment, standing orders on payday, S&S ISA direct debits
  • The employer match as the single best guaranteed return available to employed people
Intellectual grounding

Thaler & Benartzi (2004), Save More Tomorrow — empirical evidence that automatic escalation of savings rates outperforms willpower-based approaches. Nobel-grade behavioural economics applied directly.

04
Investing · Passive Income
Wealth Built While You Sleep — What That Actually Means

"Passive income" has become a content-industry scam phrase. This episode strips it back to what it actually means for most people: scalability through ownership, not side hustles — and why index investing is the most accessible version available.

Episode beats
  • The spectrum: employment → dividends → rental income → business equity
  • Why compound interest is the only genuine "while you sleep" mechanism available to most people
  • Bernstein's case for low-cost index funds vs. the active management myth
  • The real barrier: starting, not the strategy — inertia as the enemy
Intellectual grounding

Bernstein's Four Pillars of Investing on the mathematics of compounding and the evidence against active management. Vanguard's SPIVA data on fund manager underperformance as supporting evidence.

Strategy · Mind Your Own Business
Your Employer Builds Their Asset Column. Who Builds Yours?

Kiyosaki's "mind your own business" reframed for an employed audience: your employer's success accrues to shareholders, not you. Building a parallel asset column during employment is the discipline the wealthy practice and the employed rarely do.

Episode beats

  • What "owning a piece of the upside" looks like for non-business-owners: shares, equity, property
  • Employee share schemes — systematically underused in the UK
  • When to consider a Ltd structure for side income — tax and complexity trade-off
Intellectual grounding

Kiyosaki's framework, substantiated by HMRC data on employee share scheme take-up rates and the IFS on wealth accumulation patterns by employment status.

06
Debt · Leverage
Debt as a Tool — and Why That Idea Has a Body Count

Strategic leverage is a real wealth-building mechanism. It also destroyed millions of households in 2008 and again when UK rates rose from 0.1% to 5.25%. This episode teaches the principle honestly, with the risk caveat it always deserves.

Episode beats
  • Good debt vs. bad debt: the return vs. cost of borrowing test
  • Mortgage as leverage — why offset mortgages change the calculus
  • The 2022–23 rate cycle as a case study in leverage risk for landlords
  • The psychological dimension: Kahneman's loss aversion and why leverage feels worse going down
Intellectual grounding

Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky 1979) on asymmetric pain of losses. UK Finance and HMRC data on BTL landlord exits 2022–24. Bernstein on risk capacity vs. risk tolerance.

07
Tax · Legal Structures
Understand the Tax System or Pay the Maximum

The single biggest gap in the original article — and one of Kiyosaki's sharpest points. The tax code is not neutral. It systematically rewards ownership, investment, and structured income over employment. Most people never look at it.

Episode beats
  • The four legal tax reduction levers available to UK individuals: pension contributions, ISA wrapper, CGT allowance, employment structure
  • How a £60k earner can legally reduce their effective tax rate substantially via pension relief
  • Ltd company for side income: when it crosses the threshold of being worth the complexity
  • Inheritance tax planning as the final frontier most people leave too late
Intellectual grounding

HMRC tax gap data; IFS research on effective tax rates by income source (employment vs. capital). Note: always recommend professional advice for implementation — this episode frames the landscape, not the specifics.

Social Capital · Networks
It's Not Who You Know — It's the Ties You Don't Know You're Missing

The networking lesson, properly grounded. Granovetter's research shows the crucial distinction isn't between networked and not-networked — it's between strong ties (family, close friends) and weak ties (acquaintances, cross-industry contacts). The latter drive economic mobility.

Episode beats
  • Granovetter's 1973 finding: job opportunities come disproportionately from weak ties, not strong ones
  • Why working-class networks are often strong but homogeneous — high solidarity, low cross-class reach
  • How to intentionally build weak ties without feeling transactional
  • The financial value of a network: research on salary premiums for well-connected professionals
Intellectual grounding

Granovetter, The Strength of Weak Ties (1973; 1983 follow-up). Putnam, Bowling Alone on bridging vs. bonding social capital. Recent LinkedIn Economic Graph research on cross-class connections and earnings.

Negotiation · Earnings
Everything Is Negotiable — And the Gap Compounds

Negotiation isn't a confrontation skill — it's a financial one. A single salary negotiation left on the table doesn't just cost you this year. It costs you every year the raise compounds forward. The gap between those who ask and those who don't is enormous over a career.

Episode beats
  • The compound cost of a single missed salary negotiation — modelled over 20 years
  • UK-specific: mortgage affordability linked to salary, multiplying the stakes
  • Negotiation in professional fees, insurance renewal, mortgage rates — the untaught habits
  • The gender and class dimensions: who negotiates, who doesn't, and why
Intellectual grounding

Linda Babcock & Sara Laschever, Women Don't Ask — broadens to class dynamics. Research on the career earnings gap attributable to negotiation behaviour. CIPD salary benchmarking data for UK context.

Work to Learn, Not to Earn — Past the Competence Threshold

Human Capital · Insurance
Your Health Is on the Balance Sheet — Protect It Like an Asset

Reframing health from a lifestyle choice to a financial one. The human capital balance sheet: your future earning capacity has a present value — and most people leave it completely uninsured. The financial cost of incapacity is the untold story.

  • The self-employed blind spot: statutory sick pay doesn't apply to you

Association of British Insurers protection gap data. Gary Becker's human capital theory. Swiss Re and Munich Re actuarial data on income disruption from illness in working-age adults.

Risk · Resilience
Risk Management Is What Makes Risk-Taking Rational

The "fail fast" lesson, honestly told. The reason upper-class people can take calculated risks is structural — they have buffers. This episode shows how to build those buffers deliberately, so that experimentation becomes rational rather than reckless.

Episode beats
  • The emergency fund as an options contract — it buys you the right to take risk without ruin
  • Bernstein's distinction between risk tolerance (psychological) and risk capacity (financial)
  • The sequence of building buffers: emergency fund → income protection → then investment risk
  • Why "fail fast" advice without structural context is incomplete and occasionally harmful
Intellectual grounding

Bernstein's Four Pillars on risk capacity. Kahneman's prospect theory on why losses loom larger than equivalent gains — the asymmetric psychology of downside. FCA data on financial resilience across income deciles.
